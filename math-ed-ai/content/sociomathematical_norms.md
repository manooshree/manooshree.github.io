<h2 id="sociomathematical-norms">Sociomathematical Norms</h2>

<span class="purple">*Imagine all of the mathematicians in the world in one classroom: the global classroom.*</span> The professor of this course *explicitly* teaches their students about mathematical concepts and proof techniques. They also *implicitly* communicate mathematical norms and culture, important for inducting students into the practice of mathematics ([Dawkins & Weber, 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10649-016-9740-5)). As the semester goes on, the students calibrate personal understandings of the practices and norms that are accepted in the global classroom. Now, imagine the 700+ proofs OpenAI released as all being presented in one (very large) math textbook. The students in the global classroom find this textbook incomprehensible; the textbook authors used unfamiliar terminology and different ways of reasoning than they are used to. The textbook does not conform to the sociomathematical norms the students have been enculturated into, and as a result, encountering proofs that violate those expectations creates friction.


Math education researchers Erna Yackel and Paul Cobb ([1996](https://pubs.nctm.org/view/journals/jrme/27/4/article-p458.xml)) introduced <span class = "purple"> **sociomathematical norms**</span>—classroom norms that influence students’ mathematical practice. Sociomathematical norms regulate what counts as “mathematically *different*, mathematically *sophisticated*, mathematically *efficient*, and mathematically *elegant* in a classroom” ([Yackel & Cobb, 1996](https://pubs.nctm.org/view/journals/jrme/27/4/article-p458.xml)). In their seminal paper, Yackel and Cobb demonstrate how teachers and students interactively constitute and continually renegotiate the sociomathematical norms in a second-grade math classroom. Crucially, they argue that participating in and shaping the classroom's microculture fosters students' intellectual autonomy in mathematics and leads to the “development of an inquiry mathematics tradition.” Since then, many researchers have demonstrated how sociomathematical norms dictate what “counts” as a proof in a given classroom ([Carl, 2024](https://scholarship.claremont.edu/jhm/vol14/iss2/4/); [Dawkins & Weber, 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10649-016-9740-5); [Fukawa-Connelly, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmathb.2012.04.002); [Güven & Dede, 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1130899.pdf); [Stylianides, 2007](https://pubs.nctm.org/view/journals/jrme/38/3/article-p289.xml)).


Stylianides ([2007](https://pubs.nctm.org/view/journals/jrme/38/3/article-p289.xml)) takes this idea a step further, placing the classroom community at the very center of his definition of proof.  This echoes Thurston ([1994](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/0-387-29831-2_3.pdf)) in his famous "On Proof and Progress in Mathematics" essay: "proofs depend on the audience. We prove things in a social context and address them to a certain audience." For Stylianides, the audience is the classroom community, consisting primarily of students with the teacher holding special status. His definition is as follows:

1. It uses statements accepted by the *classroom community* that are true and available without further justification
2. It employs forms of reasoning that are valid and known to, or within the conceptual reach of, the *classroom community*
3. It is communicated with forms of expression that are appropriate and known to, or within the conceptual reach of, the *classroom community*. 


The second and third parts of this definition highlight how a community's sociomathematical norms constitute what counts as a valid argument. However, these norms are often implicit, making them difficult to identify outright. Carl ([2024](https://scholarship.claremont.edu/jhm/vol14/iss2/4/)) notes that sociomathematical norms become most apparent when they are violated. A recurring criticism of OpenAI's proofs that surfaces violations of sociomathematical norms has been their *poor exposition* and *unreadability*. 


<div data-include="content/norms-quotes.html"></div>


**The very idea that the released proofs are *unreadable* and have *poor exposition* implies that the mathematical community maintains a certain standard of readability and good exposition, interactively constituted** by traditional mentorship structures (such as PhD students and their advisors), workshops and conferences, and the peer review process. It may be difficult to articulate precisely what makes a mathematical proof readable, but the criticisms of OpenAI's proofs make clear that those expectations have not been met.

This brings us back to the third part of Stylianides’ definition of proof: the forms of expression used in a proof must be appropriate and within the conceptual reach of the community to which it is presented. In an essay predating the release of OpenAI’s proofs, Tao ([2026b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16753)) observes that “the spelling, grammar, and the formatting” of AI tools are nearly perfect, but “the writing very often dwells at length on trivialities while passing briefly through — or even actively obscuring — the most interesting and novel portions of the argument.” Tao echoes what Stylianides and Thurston have said before: good mathematical exposition is not merely a matter of technical correctness, but of understanding what an audience will find meaningful, interesting, and worth explaining.

In the same way that mathematicians are calling out the violations of sociomathematical norms in research papers, students are doing the same when using AI tools to assist them with their math homework. My research develops [LeanTutor](https://leantutor.org/), an AI proof tutor for undergraduate-level proofs. Hundreds of students have tried LeanTutor and the recurring piece of feedback I have heard is that students felt LeanTutor was "stingy" and "pedantic," asking for justifications of trivial statements or flagging changes in variables that students felt “a human TA would understand.” In these comments, students were demonstrating their understanding of a complete and "rigorous enough" proof in the context of their *classroom community* and reacting to LeanTutor's violations of those norms.

Below, I share Vincent's experience using LeanTutor, which was representative of the many students who accepted or challenged the system's feedback based not on its mathematical accuracy, but on whether they considered it appropriate. I additionally chose to highlight Vincent's experience because he was particularly cognizant that sociomathematical norms vary across different classrooms. 


This interview with Vincent was collected as part of a larger study evaluating the educational impacts and user experience of LeanTutor. In Spring 2026, LeanTutor was deployed in EECS127, an upper-division linear algebra and optimization-based course, and used by about 200 students. Our research team conducted semi-structured clinical interviews with 27 students (such as Vincent), observing them complete 2–3 proofs in the LeanTutor system over the course of two hours. An HCI-focused analysis of all user interviews can be found in Guo et al. ([2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.00760)). 

<span class="dark-purple">**Transcript Summary**:</span> Vincent completes three proofs during the interview (proofs two and three are two parts of the same theorem). He completes the first "warmup" proof on the first try. He also completes the second proof on his first attempt. After the second proof, LeanTutor provides optional proof improvements; the system suggests adding more justification for why his chosen vector satisfies the problem constraints. Vincent says he would have probably skipped through these if he were at home, but after reading through them, he agrees with one of the suggestions and even expresses surprise that LeanTutor did not reject his proof for omitting the detail. Despite beginning the interview with the prior that LeanTutor's feedback was unnecessarily rigorous, Vincent was willing to consider and incorporate the suggestions.

Vincent attempts the third proof four times, until it is marked correct. On his second attempt, the system flags his first step because it is insufficiently justified. But Vincent felt "like it's a given" and grounds his challenge of LeanTutor in how the instructors' solutions are typically written. Vincent evaluates LeanTutor's feedback against his understanding of the classroom's expectations of proof rigor, instead of unquestioningly accepting the correction.  

At the end of the interview, Vincent reflects on the role of proof in different classrooms. Vincent says that in EECS127, "the proofs don't matter as much as like the results." He contrasts this with CS70, a discrete math and probability course in which first- and second-year computer science students are introduced to and expected to write rigorous proofs, which he claims is "very like theory-oriented." Vincent demonstrates a sensitivity to the varying standards, or sociomathematical norms, of different courses and the different role a proof plays in the context of each course. Through these interactions, Vincent is not a passive recipient of LeanTutor's assessments, but maintains his agency when negotiating—not the accuracy—but the appropriateness of LeanTutor's feedback.

### Human-AI Interaction in Mathematics

The LeanTutor system is a research-level prototype and as someone who took EECS127 years ago, I do agree with students that its feedback was sometimes unnecessarily rigorous. Mindful of these limitations, the interviewers informed students that LeanTutor was still under development and could make mistakes. We believe this encouraged students to openly challenge LeanTutor's suggestions. This gave the interviewers an insightful window into one of the most difficult aspects of writing proofs for students: knowing when their proof is "enough." It also showcased a model of human-AI interaction in mathematics in which the human retained their agency and organically incorporated the AI tool into their existing mathematical practices and community norms.

This model of human-AI interaction presents an alternative way OpenAI could have generated their "textbook" of 700+ proofs for the *global classroom*. Rather than "dumping" hundreds of completed proofs for mathematicians to evaluate post hoc, they could have provided model access to the experts already working on the relevant research programs. Mathematicians would have brought their field's norms of constructing and communicating the proofs. This approach would have likely produced proofs that were more easily understood, critically evaluated, and built upon by the math community. 

Looking forward, AI tools will reflexively shape the norms and practices of many communities, much as they have transformed the practice of software engineering with more code being written than ever before ([Favaro & Clark, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)). Similarly, Terry Tao argues that we are in "the era of proof abundance," in stark contrast to Math 1.0, the traditional pre-AI era of mathematics characterized by proof scarcity ([Tao, 2026a](https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/slides/math-2-0-caltech-2026.pdf)). In light of this abundance, mathematicians are rethinking what it means to do mathematics and Tao proposes the Math 2.0 framework, which emphasizes mathematical understanding over problem solving. Indeed, we are already seeing mathematicians renegotiate sociomathematical norms in light of AI-generated mathematics. Axel Boldt ([2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.29401)) builds on the [Leiden Declaration](https://leidendeclaration.ai/) and proposes three *new norms* he believes authors who extensively use LLMs to generate mathematical work must adhere to.
