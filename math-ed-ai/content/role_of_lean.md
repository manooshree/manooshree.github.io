<h2 id="epistemic-role-of-lean">The Epistemic Role of Lean</h2>


<blockquote class="epigraph">
<p>“If your code compiles, the mathematics is correct.” <span class="epigraph-cite">— <a href="https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/02/09/accelerating-mathematics/">Kevin Buzzard, 2026</a></span></p>
</blockquote>

When OpenAI announced their solution to the Navier-Stokes problem, many mathematicians did not understand the informal writeup, but the accompanying Lean proof left "little doubt now that the solution is correct" ([Avigad et al., 2026](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/10/16/how-to-formalize-all-of-mathematics/)). Lean's correctness guarantee provides so much conviction that "Lean-pilled" readers of LLM-generated math papers are not deterred by incomprehensible writeups, as long as a Lean proof is present ([Howlett, 2026](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/mathematicians-marvel-and-grumble-at-openais-trove-of-new-results/)). As formal verification of math proofs becomes cheaper due to LLMs' increasing autoformalization and proving capabilities, the long-standing vision of formalizing all of mathematics becomes more feasible. But mathematical writeups that humans do not understand, accompanied by massive formalizations spanning 13M ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/formalizing-fermats-last-theorem)) or even 43M lines of code ([Axiom, 2026](https://axiommath.ai/research/the-enormous-theorem-formalized/)) that humans cannot read, provide little signal outside of theorem correctness. 

Taking the existence of a Lean proof as evidence that a theorem is true is akin to a student accepting that a theorem is true simply because their professor wrote it on the blackboard. Math education researchers Guershon Harel and Larry Sowder ([1998](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~harel/Students%27%20Proof%20Schemes.pdf)) would say the student is using an "authoritarian proof scheme": a type of *external proof scheme* in which the student accepts something as mathematically correct because an external authority figure declared it as such. If mathematics were merely *prescribed* in this way, students would have little opportunity to develop personal mathematical meanings for themselves ([Thompson, 2013](https://pat-thompson.net/PDFversions/2013AbsenceMeaning.pdf)). Math educators strive to foster curious and intellectually independent students who can question mathematical claims, develop their own arguments, and contribute to mathematical knowledge. In both scenarios, a Lean proof telling us Navier-Stokes is correct and a professor telling her student a theorem is correct, it is inarguably valuable to know that a theorem statement is true, but we should aspire to go beyond authoritative guarantees. **What role do these formalizations have outside of solely establishing correctness?**


For the past few months, I have been questioning how the epistemic role of a Lean proof will evolve in mathematics practice. So far, that role seems mostly confined to verification of theorems and catching mistakes in published math papers ([Ilin, 2026](https://x.com/IlinVasily29521/status/2117337286421725688)). However, mathematicians have pointed out that if the theorem was already accepted by the math community, then "the value of a formalization comes from our ability to use and interact with the data" ([Avigad et al., 2026](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/10/16/how-to-formalize-all-of-mathematics/)). In 2021, Peter Scholze described how the formalization process of the Liquid Tensor Experiment, particularly interacting with the small team of Lean experts working on the formalization, helped him clarify parts of his own proof he was not sure of ([Scholze, 2021](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2021/06/05/half-a-year-of-the-liquid-tensor-experiment-amazing-developments/)). Lean proofs can provide so much more to math practice than just verification or error-catching—I believe they can help with understanding and clarifying steps in proofs. To this end, Harel and Sowder's ([1998](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~harel/Students%27%20Proof%20Schemes.pdf)) taxonomy of proof schemes is a useful map delineating the different ways in which people develop conviction in a proof. 

### Harel & Sowder's Proof Schemes

Guershon Harel and Larry Sowder are two prominent proof educators who introduced the highly influential notion of proof schemes to help classify how students gain conviction in correct (and incorrect) proofs ([Harel & Sowder, 1998](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~harel/Students%27%20Proof%20Schemes.pdf)). They say, 

<blockquote class="epigraph in-text">
<p>“A person's proof scheme consists of what constitutes ascertaining and persuading for that person.”</p>
</blockquote>

 Harel and Sowder ([1998](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~harel/Students%27%20Proof%20Schemes.pdf)) define *ascertaining* as convincing oneself of a proof and *persuading* as convincing others of a proof, and consider these the two subprocesses of proving. Through observing 128 teaching experiments with mostly undergraduate students, Harel and Sowder develop 16 empirically grounded subcategories of proof schemes. 

They propose three categories of proof schemes: <a class="green" href="#scheme-external">external conviction</a>, <a class="green" href="#scheme-empirical">empirical</a>, and <a class="green" href="#scheme-analytical">analytical</a>. While these categories are not hierarchical, instructors value them differently. They do not want students to rely solely on external conviction proof schemes; they see empirical proof schemes as developmentally helpful but not always sufficient; and they aim to help students develop analytical proof schemes to support their mathematical development. These schemes are also not mutually exclusive and a student will likely engage with a proof using many schemes simultaneously. 

<figure class="scheme-tree" id="scheme-external">
<img src="images/proof-schemes-external.png" alt="External conviction proof schemes: ritual, authoritarian, symbolic.">
<figcaption>External conviction proof schemes (Harel &amp; Sowder, 1998).</figcaption>
</figure>

 When students maintain an **external proof scheme** to gain conviction in a proof, they believe a proof is true because: 1) the appearance of the argument is ritualistic (e.g., a math proof usually will have symbols in it, so a proof with no symbols is not a real proof), 2) an authority claimed it was true (e.g., a theorem appears in a textbook), or 3) the symbolic form of the argument (e.g., manipulating symbols without understanding the problem). Accepting a theorem as true because a compiling Lean proof exists could use an authoritarian proof scheme. Harel and Sowder ([1998](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~harel/Students%27%20Proof%20Schemes.pdf)) note that sometimes, an *authoritarian proof scheme* is necessary when a student is introduced to completely new content. However, generally when students use external proof schemes, they "are unlikely to gain confidence in their ability to create mathematics" ([Harel & Sowder, 1998](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~harel/Students%27%20Proof%20Schemes.pdf)).

<figure class="scheme-tree" id="scheme-empirical">
<img src="images/proof-schemes-empirical.png" alt="Empirical proof schemes: inductive, perceptual.">
<figcaption>Empirical proof schemes (Harel &amp; Sowder, 1998).</figcaption>
</figure>

**Empirical proof schemes** emerge when students are convinced by a proof through “appeals to physical facts or sensory experiences” ([Harel & Sowder, 1998](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~harel/Students%27%20Proof%20Schemes.pdf)). Students possess an *inductive proof scheme* when they use one or more specific examples to convince themselves of a theorem. When using a *perceptual proof scheme*, a student will perceive the objects, but not the mathematical relationships between them. For example, a student may believe a triangle has two equal sides because it looks like that in the figure, rather than deductively establishing that the triangle is isosceles from its angle measurements.

<figure class="scheme-tree" id="scheme-analytical">
<img src="images/proof-schemes-analytical.png" alt="Analytical proof schemes: transformational (internalized, interiorized, restrictive, with contextual, generic, constructive and spatial) and axiomatic (intuitive-axiomatic, structural, axiomatizing).">
<figcaption>Analytical proof schemes (Harel &amp; Sowder, 1998).</figcaption>
</figure>

**Analytical proof schemes** encompass the spirit of mathematical proof, applying logical deductions to reach a conclusion. Harel and Sowder ([1998](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~harel/Students%27%20Proof%20Schemes.pdf)) identify two high-level subcategories: *transformational* and *axiomatic* proof schemes. When students perform goal-oriented transformations to objects in a proof and anticipate their consequences, they are engaging in the proof via a *transformational proof scheme*. For example, a student reasoning about the rank of a matrix may transform it into reduced row-echelon form, recognizing that this transformation preserves rank and this form may be more conducive to accomplishing the proof's goal. On the other hand, an *axiomatic proof scheme* involves understanding that mathematical statements are derived from a set of basic axioms and undefined terms. 

<strong class="green">I believe Lean proofs can be engaged with in a way that provides conviction of a theorem's correctness not only through the external authoritarian proof scheme, but through the analytical proof schemes.</strong> This would shift a Lean proof's role beyond verification to helping mathematicians (and students) develop personal meanings and further their understanding of the proof. Scholze's account is an exemplar of how Lean can assist someone in better understanding their own mathematical argument ([Scholze, 2021](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2021/06/05/half-a-year-of-the-liquid-tensor-experiment-amazing-developments/)). 

I observed this when talking to Nick, another student who participated in a LeanTutor interview. For Nick, LeanTutor's repeated assessments that a step he doubted in his proof was indeed correct were insufficient for him to gain conviction in the step. I highlight Nick's experience below because I found him to be particularly earnest in his efforts to ascertain the proof step for himself, when he could have just accepted the Lean verification signal authoritatively. 

I report the first half of Nick's interview below. 

<span class="dark-green">**Transcript Summary**:</span> As he writes his initial proof on the whiteboard, he expresses doubts about the validity of bounding all the $x_i^2$ terms by the max $x_1^2$ term and whether the $1/n$ factor belongs inside or outside of the square root. This step is [boxed in green](#nick-whiteboard) in his proof. He concretizes his doubt by telling the interviewers his confidence that his proof is correct is “probably a three or four" out of five because he thinks he "lost a factor of square root of somewhere." After the first submission, LeanTutor flags an error in the proof that is different from the step he is unsure of. Nick fixes the proof and continues to think through the unsure step. LeanTutor assesses his step as completely correct after the second verification attempt. Once Nick submits the proof for credit, the interviewers direct him to look at the step in doubt in the Lean code. As Nick has never interacted with Lean before, the interviewers spend a few minutes explaining what theorem statements and tactics are and how to interact with the Lean Infoview. Nick reads the in-line comments in the Lean code and as he does, his changing tone of voice demonstrates his growing conviction that he is indeed correct. 

<blockquote class="epigraph in-text">
<p>“So I was right that you need the absolute values because otherwise you're working in the whole $x^2$ domain which is the parabolic but parab- parabolas by themselves aren't monotonic.” <span class="epigraph-cite">— Nick</span></p>
</blockquote>

Interestingly, viewing the Lean code appeared to support Nick's confidence in the proof step, but the justification he settled on is one he had been developing through the interview about monotonic functions—not one the Lean code provided. There are two noteworthy things about the role the Lean code plays for Nick. Firstly, despite the step in doubt never being marked incorrect by LeanTutor, Nick repeatedly questions its accuracy. We cannot conclude for sure whether this is because Nick did not have trust in the LeanTutor system to be accurate or whether using an authoritarian proof scheme (of a Lean correctness signal) was insufficient for him. However, his repeated ponderings about the correctness of his move throughout the interview make me believe it is the latter. Secondly, the Lean code seemed to convince Nick that his step was correct, but he did not adopt Lean's justification as to why. From the transcripts, Nick appears to have had some mental image of a parabola with only non-negative values that was a monotonic function, and he used the Lean code as a stepping-stone to reinforce this personal argument. The use of the Lean code supplemented his path to ascertaining, but did not replace him developing a personal meaning for himself.  


Nick's interactions with LeanTutor and Scholze's ([2021](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2021/06/05/half-a-year-of-the-liquid-tensor-experiment-amazing-developments/)) involvement in the formalization of his proof showcase how intentional engagement with Lean code, even if you are not the one who wrote the Lean code, can still help in gaining personal conviction about your own mathematical arguments. Their experiences display that a Lean proof is not something that can only be interacted with through an authoritarian proof scheme, but perhaps also through *transformational* and *axiomatic* schemes. Engaging with proofs using these schemes contributes positively to personal mathematical development and helps the student, or even the Fields Medal winner, become an even better mathematician. In both Nick's and Scholze's cases, their interaction with the Lean code was scaffolded by talking to folks who understood the code. My co-interviewer, Nora Guo, and I mediated Nick's interaction with the Lean code, and Johan Commelin (the primary Lean formalizer, alongside a larger team) mediated Scholze's, helping them resolve their confusions or develop their arguments. Unfortunately, not all Lean code can come with a human translator, so **can we operationalize this scaffolding in Lean code to help users use the Lean code to ascertain their own arguments**? I believe the time is now to build interfaces that do this (though Peter Scholze also [said this](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2021/06/05/half-a-year-of-the-liquid-tensor-experiment-amazing-developments/) in 2021!) and this was one of our primary aims when building LeanTutor. Such interfaces could support people in navigating the Lean code of their own mathematical arguments, even when they haven't generated the code themselves, especially considering how easy it has become to autoformalize arguments with LLMs. Who knows what mathematical insights people might uncover by interacting with formalizations of their own arguments!

<h3 class="analogy" aria-label="Summary">Summary</h3>

Proof education researchers have spent decades developing frameworks and definitions explaining how students construct, understand, and verify proofs. These same frameworks and definitions can also help make sense of the mathematical community's reactions to AI-generated proofs. Criticisms of OpenAI's proofs reveal that correctness alone is not enough: for more mathematicians to embrace AI-generated proofs, these proofs must adhere to the community's sociomathematical norms, norms that humans like Vincent actively negotiate rather than passively accept. And while Lean is currently primarily used as a *sledgehammer* to demonstrate correctness, Nick's and Scholze's experiences show that engaging with Lean code can go past that and help someone gain conviction in, and understanding of, their own mathematical arguments.
